Can people find Ruth Elliott & Co online?

We looked at your website the way Google and a first-time visitor do. Here is what we found, in plain English, with no jargon.

4 of 10

Your site passes 4 of the 10 basic checks. The foundations are sound: it is secure, quick and open to Google. The gaps are all about how the practice is described to search engines.

The one that matters most

Google is told your name, but not what you do.

Your page title, the line Google shows in blue, reads "Ruth Elliott & Co" and nothing else. The page has no main heading, and no description for Google to show underneath.

Most of your new clients come by recommendation, so the first thing they do is look you up. What they see in the results is a name with no mention of chartered accountants or Northwich, and a few lines Google has picked from the page itself. The words are all there further down the page; they are just not in the three places a search engine looks first.

Five smaller things to fix

None of these is difficult, and a new site would put all of them right.

To fix

No description for search results

A website can hand Google two lines to show under its name. Yours does not, so Google chooses its own from the page.

A new site would say who you are, what you cover and where, in your own words.

To fix

No main heading on the page

Search engines read a page's main heading to work out what it is about. Your page has none. Its first headings are your name and "Your numbers, our strength", neither of which says accountancy.

A new site would open with a heading that names the practice as chartered accountants in Northwich.

To fix

Shared links have a picture but no description

When someone passes your link on in a message or an email, your logo appears, which is good. No line of description goes with it.

A new site would send a short description with every shared link.

To fix

The pictures are not described

Pictures can carry a short written description, which search engines read and which screen readers speak aloud. The four pictures on your page, including your portrait and your logo, have none.

A new site would describe each picture, starting with "Ruth Elliott FCA" on your portrait.

To fix

No fact sheet for Google

A website can give Google a small fact sheet: the practice's name, address, phone number and what kind of business it is. Yours does not have one, so Google has to work those out from the page.

A new site would include one, so your details are read correctly.

Can AI assistants find you?

More people now ask ChatGPT, Claude or Google's AI answers instead of searching. These tools read your website too. We checked four things they rely on. Your site passes 3 of 4.

Working

Nothing on your site turns them away.

Working

Your words are there in the page itself, so AI tools can read them.

Working

Both phone numbers, your email address and your Hartford address are on the page as text.

To fix

A summary file for AI tools

There is none. It is a newer idea, a short plain-text summary of what the practice does and who for, and it takes about twenty minutes to write.

One small thing we noticed: on a phone, your mobile number can be tapped to call, but the office number cannot.

Nobody can make an AI recommend a business, and anyone who offers to is selling nothing. What a website can do is let these tools reach it, read it and get your facts right.
